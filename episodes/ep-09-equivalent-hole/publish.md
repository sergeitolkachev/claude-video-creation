REAL, AND CHECKABLE

Choked flow. Air escaping through a small hole into vacuum reaches the speed of sound in the hole, and from then on the flow depends only on the pressure inside: m = Cd x A x p x sqrt(g/(R T)) x (2/(g+1))^((g+1)/(2(g-1))). For air at 20 C (g = 1.4, R = 287, T = 293) with Cd = 0.62 for a sharp-edged hole, that is m = 0.62 x 0.002361 x A x p.

Day one. A 1.4 mm hole at 101.3 kPa loses 2.28e-4 kg/s, 19.7 kg a day. In 140 m3 (168.7 kg of air) that is a time constant of 8.55 days, and four hours of decay is 1.96 kPa. A 300 kg reserve covers 15.2 days.

Finding it. Closing hatches and timing the decay in each section is the standard way to locate a leak; a real station crew did exactly this in 2020. The 30 m3 module with the hole falls 2.31 kPa an hour; the others hold. Ultrasonic leak detectors listen around 40 kHz, where escaping gas is loud and nothing else is.

Correcting for pressure. In a sealed volume the leak slows as the pressure falls, but the pressure half-life depends only on the hole: 30.5 h at 1.4 mm, 49 h at 1.1 mm, 166 h at 0.6 mm in that module. Integrated from day one, the module reads 42.3 kPa on day three and 27.9 kPa on day five, and stops at 27.7 kPa when the hole reaches zero.

A leak does not sort gases. Choked flow passes the mixture as it is, so the oxygen fraction inside a leaking sealed volume stays at 20.9%.

Equalisation. Opening 30 m3 at 27.7 kPa to 110 m3 at 101.3 kPa settles at 85.5 kPa; restoring the whole station to 101.3 kPa takes 26.2 kg of makeup - the same mass the module lost. A hatch between sections at different pressures cannot be opened until a valve has levelled them.
