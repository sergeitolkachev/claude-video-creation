## Disclaimer

> This record is fiction. The physics is real and checks: the fall of air pressure with height, the twice-daily pressure tide of the tropics, the size limit that breathing through tubes puts on insects, and the published figures for the largest living and fossil dragonflies. The basin is modelled on a real salt lake 155 m below sea level, but its station, its barometer record and its cyclone are invented. The animal and every figure attached to it are invented. Details at the bottom.

## Synopsis

On the slopes of a closed salt basin whose lake lies 155 m below sea level
lives an odonate with a 68 cm wingspan. Every specimen has been found below
sea level. Of 2,300 radar tracks the highest reached 26 m below the sea.

An insect has no lungs. Air reaches its muscle through tubes, and the last of
the way by diffusion, which is why the largest living members of its order
span 19 cm. The 70 cm giants of the early Permian flew in air of about 30%
oxygen. In beetles the tubes into each leg take a larger share of the joint
the larger the body. This animal's legs carry almost none: it does not walk,
it hangs. And its tubes run straight through the flight muscle and out, with
the abdomen driving air through them one way — 40 strokes a minute at rest,
three a second in flight, which is its limit.

At that limit, flight needs air at 1,014.5 hPa. Oxygen is a fifth of the air
at every height; only the amount of air changes, about 1% per 90 m. At the
water's edge on an ordinary day the air holds 1.3% more oxygen than flight
needs. At sea level it would not fly at all. Its ceiling is 40 m below the sea.

The ceiling is weather. Twice a day the tropical atmosphere swells and
slackens by about a hectopascal, and the ceiling moves between 29 and 51 m
below the sea; the tracks follow it. The shore barometer has run 61 years. Its
lowest reading put the ceiling at 112 m below the sea — 43 m above the water.
No cyclone in that record has entered the basin.

On Sunday a cyclone forms in the Gulf. The forecast minimum for the basin on
Thursday is 995 hPa. At that pressure the ceiling is about twenty metres below
the surface of the lake.

The one invented thing: an insect that pumps air through its flight muscle,
and so can be large in today's air, but only where the air is heavy enough.

## Voice direction

Narrator. Unhurried, third person, present tense.

- The record is a survey. Scenes 1–5 describe an animal and its air; scene 6
  is a station record; scene 7 is a forecast read off a bulletin. Read all
  seven identically.
- "It does not walk. It hangs." — four words, two facts, no image in the
  delivery.

  specification, not a surprise.
- Hectopascals are read plainly, at the same speed as everything else.

## Appendix — what is real and what is invented

### Real, verifiable

- **Oxygen fraction.** Dry air is 20.95% oxygen by volume, and that fraction
  is the same at every height in the lower atmosphere. What changes is the
  pressure, and with it the partial pressure of oxygen.
- **Pressure with height.** Hydrostatic: dP/dz = −ρg. Air at 34 °C and about
  1,020 hPa has ρ ≈ 1.16 kg/m³, so pressure changes 1.14 hPa per 10 m, and
  the scale height is H = R·T/g = 287.05 × 307.15 / 9.807 = 8,991 m. One per
  cent of the air, and so of the oxygen, every 90 m.
- **The pressure tide.** Solar heating drives a semidiurnal oscillation of
  surface pressure, largest in the tropics: an amplitude of about 1–1.5 hPa,
  maxima near 10:00 and 22:00 local time, minima near 04:00 and 16:00. The
  record uses ±1.2 hPa.
- **Why insects stay small.** Insects carry oxygen to their tissue through a
  tracheal system; larger insects ventilate the main tubes by abdominal
  pumping (locusts, for example), but the last step, through the tracheoles
  into the cells, is diffusion. In darkling beetles the tracheae entering the
  leg grow faster with body size than the leg opening that carries them
  (exponents about 1.02 against 0.77; Kaiser et al., PNAS 2007), and the
  extrapolation runs out of room at about the size of the largest living
  beetle, *Titanus giganteus* (~17 cm).
- **The living and fossil odonates.** The largest living member of the order,
  the damselfly *Megaloprepus caerulatus*, spans about 19 cm. *Meganeuropsis
  permiana* (early Permian, Kansas) spanned about 70 cm, 68–75 cm by specimen
  and reconstruction. Atmospheric oxygen in the late Carboniferous and early
  Permian is estimated at about 30%, with published estimates running 23–35%.
  The oxygen explanation for the giants is debated; the record says
  "estimated" and does not claim it as settled.
- **The basin it is modelled on.** Lake Assal, Djibouti: a closed hypersaline
  lake about 155 m below sea level, the lowest point in Africa, in the Afar
  rift near the Gulf of Aden. The record does not name it, because the
  station, the barometer record and the animal are not real.
- **Cyclones in the Gulf of Aden are rare but real.** Cyclone Sagar (May 2018)
  reached about 994 hPa and brought flooding to Djibouti and Somalia.

### Invented

- **The animal**: a 68 cm odonate whose tracheae run through the flight muscle
  and out, ventilated one way by the abdomen, so that the diffusion step is
  short enough for its size. Its legs carry almost no tracheae; it does not
  walk.
- **Its pump**: 40 strokes a minute at rest, 3 a second in flight, and 3 a
  second as its limit.
- **Its flight threshold**: 1,014.5 hPa of total pressure, i.e. an oxygen
  partial pressure of 21.25 kPa — just above the 21.23 kPa of a standard sea
  level day (1,013.25 hPa).
- **The station**: 2,300 radar tracks, highest −26 m; a shore barometer with
  a 61-year record and a lowest reading of 1,002.0 hPa; an ordinary-day
  sea-level pressure of 1,010.0 hPa over the basin.
- **The cyclone** and its forecast minimum of 995.0 hPa on Thursday.

### The arithmetic, so it can be checked

All pressures are reduced to sea level, and the ceiling is the height at which
the local pressure falls to 1,014.5 hPa: z = −H · ln(1,014.5 / P_sea), with
H = 8,991 m.

- **Ordinary day.** P_sea = 1,010.0. Ceiling = −8,991 × ln(1.00446) = **−40 m**.
  At the water (−155 m): 1,010.0 × e^(155/8,991) = **1,027.6 hPa**, which is
  1.29% above 1,014.5 — "one point three per cent more than flight needs".
- **At sea level it could not fly.** 1,010.0 < 1,014.5, and so is the standard
  atmosphere's 1,013.25.
- **The tide.** 1,010.0 ± 1.2 hPa: at 1,011.2 the ceiling is **−29 m**, at
  1,008.8 it is **−51 m**. A 21.4 m swing (22 m between the rounded figures on
  the card), twice a day.
- **The highest track.** −26 m needs P_sea = 1,011.6 — the 10:00 crest of a day
  0.4 hPa above ordinary. Consistent with 2,300 tracks over a season.
- **The record low.** P_sea = 1,002.0: ceiling **−112 m** (−111.5), **43 m**
  above the water.
- **Thursday.** P_sea = 995.0: ceiling **−175 m** (−174.5), **about twenty
  metres** (19.5) below the lake surface at −155 m. For the ceiling to reach
  the water, sea-level pressure has to fall to 997.2 hPa; the record low was
  1,002.0, and Sagar's central pressure was about 994.
- **Consistency.** Every ceiling in the record comes from the same threshold
  and the same H. Changing the threshold moves all six ceilings together;
  changing the temperature (and so H) moves them by about 0.3% per degree.
