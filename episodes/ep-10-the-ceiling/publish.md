REAL, AND CHECKABLE

Oxygen is 20.95% of dry air at every height in the lower atmosphere. What changes with height is the pressure, and with it the amount of oxygen in each breath.

Pressure with height: dP/dz = -rho g. Air at 34 C and about 1,020 hPa has rho = 1.16 kg/m3, so pressure changes 1.14 hPa every 10 m. The scale height is H = R T / g = 287.05 x 307.15 / 9.807 = 8,991 m: one per cent of the air for every 90 m.

The pressure tide: solar heating drives a twice-daily swing of surface pressure, largest in the tropics - about 1 to 1.5 hPa, highest near 10:00 and 22:00 local time, lowest near 04:00 and 16:00. This record uses 1.2 hPa.

Why insects stay small: their tissue is supplied by a tracheal system. Larger insects pump air into the main tubes (locusts do, with the abdomen), but the last step into the muscle is diffusion. In darkling beetles the tubes entering the leg grow faster with body size than the opening that carries them (exponents about 1.02 against 0.77, Kaiser et al., PNAS 2007), and at about the size of the largest living beetle, Titanus giganteus (~17 cm), the extrapolation runs out of room.

The largest living member of the dragonfly order, the damselfly Megaloprepus caerulatus, spans about 19 cm. Meganeuropsis permiana, from the early Permian of Kansas, spanned about 70 cm (68-75 by specimen and reconstruction). Oxygen then is estimated at about 30%, with published estimates from 23 to 35%. That oxygen explains the giants is a hypothesis, not a settled fact.

The basin is modelled on Lake Assal in Djibouti: a closed hypersaline lake about 155 m below sea level, the lowest point in Africa, near the Gulf of Aden. Cyclones in the Gulf of Aden are rare but real; Cyclone Sagar (May 2018) reached about 994 hPa and flooded parts of Djibouti and Somalia.

INVENTED

The animal: a 68 cm odonate whose tubes run through the flight muscle and out, ventilated in one direction by the abdomen. Its legs carry almost no tubes, so it does not walk. It pumps 40 strokes a minute at rest and 3 a second in flight, which is its limit. At that limit flight needs air at 1,014.5 hPa - an oxygen pressure of 21.25 kPa, just above a standard day at sea level (21.23).

The station: 2,300 radar tracks, the highest at -26 m. A shore barometer with 61 years of record and a lowest reading of 1,002.0 hPa. An ordinary day of 1,010.0 hPa. The cyclone and its forecast of 995.0 hPa on Thursday.

THE ARITHMETIC

All pressures are at sea level. The ceiling is the height at which pressure falls to 1,014.5 hPa: z = -H ln(1,014.5 / P), with H = 8,991 m.

Ordinary day, 1,010.0 hPa: ceiling -40 m. At the water (-155 m) the air is at 1,027.6 hPa, 1.3% above what flight needs. At sea level it would not fly at all - not even on a standard day of 1,013.25.

The tide, 1,010.0 +/- 1.2: ceiling -29 m at the morning and evening crest, -51 m at the trough.

The highest track, -26 m, needs 1,011.6 hPa: the 10:00 crest of a day slightly above ordinary.

Record low, 1,002.0: ceiling -112 m, 43 m above the water.

Thursday, 995.0: ceiling -175 m, about twenty metres below the surface of the lake. The ceiling reaches the water at 997.2 hPa.
